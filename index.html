The Emotional Reality of Founder Finances

The hardest part of founder finance isn't the numbers — it's what the numbers do to your head. This worksheet helps you recognize the three patterns that derail founder decisions in year one, and pre-commit to how you'll respond when the financial weather turns.

How to use this worksheet: Work through it while things are calm. The decisions you make here protect you when they aren't. Your answers save automatically.
Part 1

What's Happening to You vs. What's Happening to the Business

When the financial weather turns, two problems show up at once — and they feel like the same problem from the inside. Founders who make it learn to separate them. Start by naming what makes the difference hard for you.

When you imagine a hard month — revenue down, cash tight — what's the first thing that happens in your head?
Not what you'd want to do. What you actually feel. Be honest. Nobody's reading this but you.
Who in your life will feel the weight of those moments alongside you?
A spouse, kids, a co-founder, early employees. Naming them now changes how seriously you take what comes next.
Part 2

The Three Patterns That Derail Founders

For each of the three patterns, rate how vulnerable you think you are — and write down where you've already seen it in yourself, in this venture or a past role. The honest answer is the useful one.

1
Panic-Cutting
Feels like decisiveness. The calm is the trap.
When revenue drops, the instinct is to cut hard and fast. Some of those cuts are right and well-timed. Others get made because cutting feels like doing something — and doing something feels better than sitting with the fear. The cuts that are easiest to make are often the ones most expensive to undo.
"Cutting under panic is not the same as cutting under a plan. From the outside they look identical. From the inside, only one includes the question: what is this cut going to cost me to undo?"
How vulnerable are you to panic-cutting?
Where have you already seen this in yourself?
A past role. A current decision. A budget cut that felt productive but you can't quite defend.
2
Avoidance
The numbers don't get better in private.
There are stretches where you won't want to look at the bank account. You'll know what's in it. You won't want to see it confirmed. Looking doesn't change the number — but somehow not looking makes it feel like maybe, somehow, it'll be different next time. It won't.
"If you only look at your numbers when you feel ready to look at them, you have a problem you don't know about yet."
How vulnerable are you to financial avoidance?
Where have you already seen this in yourself?
Personal finances count too. Credit card statements you let pile up. A bill you stopped opening. A metric at work you stopped checking.
3
Impatience
Makes wrong deals look reasonable.
You'll keep thinking you should already be through it. Three months in. Six. Nine. You'll have a timeline in your head about how long recovery should take, and reality will keep failing to meet it. That gap will start to feel like a personal failure instead of just what's happening — and that's when bad deals start to look like good ones.
"Impatience doesn't make you take stupid deals. It makes deals that should look stupid look reasonable, because sitting still feels worse than being wrong."
How vulnerable are you to impatience?
Where have you already seen this in yourself?
A deal you took because you needed proof of progress. A hire you rushed. A pivot you announced before you'd thought it through.
Part 3

Which One Is Yours?

All three patterns will visit you. One of them is most likely to drive the decision you'll regret. Naming it now is half the work of catching it later.

Of the three patterns, which is most likely to be yours?
Not the one that sounds worst. The one that, if you're being honest, you've already seen in yourself.
What will tip you off that this pattern is showing up — before the decision is made?
A physical signal, a phrase you tell yourself, a kind of email you start writing. The earlier you can name it, the better.
Part 4

Three Commitments You Make Now

These are the lesson's three commitments — written down while things are calm, so they're already in place when they aren't. They are the most important thing on this page.

Commitment 1 · The Cost-to-Undo Rule
Before any cut, you'll ask: what would it cost me — in time and money — to put this back?
If you can't answer that question, you're not cutting. You're flinching. Write your version of the rule below — in language that will land for you in the moment.
Commitment 2 · Your Named Person
When the financial weather turns, who is the one person you'll talk to?
A spouse. A co-founder. An advisor. A former boss. One person. Named. Pick now — you won't pick well in the moment.
Commitment 3 · The Numbers Schedule
What day and time, every week, will you look at the numbers — whether you want to or not?
Cash position, receivables, payables, runway. Twenty minutes. Especially the weeks you don't want to.
Part 5

The Three-Year Zoom-Out

In a twelve-month window, every decision looks like either a triumph or a disaster — and impatience thrives in that picture. In a three-year window, most of what feels catastrophic in month four doesn't even show up. Practice the zoom-out now, on something that feels urgent right now.

What's the financial decision or worry that feels most urgent today?
Be specific. A number, a deadline, a deal, a hire.
Now — three years from today — will this even be on the page?
Most of what makes you impatient today won't be. Some of it absolutely will. Be honest about which this is.
Knowing that, what does the right decision look like?
No need to commit to it here. Just notice how the answer changes when the window does.
Your Assignment

Three Things Before You Move On

The three commitments above only protect you if they leave this worksheet and enter your week. Pull each one into your real life — calendar, contacts, a place you'll actually see them.

1. Put your numbers schedule on your calendar
Make it recurring. Twenty minutes, weekly, the day and time you committed to above. Write below what the calendar entry will say.
2. Send a short note to your named person
Tell them you've named them as the person you'll call when the financial weather turns. Draft the note here — short, clear, and true.
3. Write the cost-to-undo rule somewhere you'll see it
A note on your desk. The top of your weekly review doc. The lock screen on your phone. Where will yours live?
